# Discussion

This chapter answers the research questions, compares the results with previous research, and assesses the reliability, validity and ethics of the work. It then discusses the applicability of the results, proposes further development, and reviews the thesis process and the author's professional growth.

## Answers to the research questions

**RQ1: How can a serverless AWS architecture be designed to turn data center IoT telemetry into safe, automated remediation actions?** The thesis showed that this can be done with a small set of core services: IoT Core for device connectivity and commands, a topic rule for routing, a stateless Lambda function for detection, EventBridge for decoupling detection from response, Step Functions for the remediation workflow, and DynamoDB for telemetry, incidents, locks and guardrail state. Three design choices proved essential. First, device shadows give commands a durable, auditable and idempotent path back to the equipment. Second, one generic workflow with playbooks defined as data keeps every remediation on the same safe pattern of lock, precheck, approval, action, verification and rollback. Third, the guardrails make the automation's limits explicit: high-risk actions always wait for a person, every action is verified on fresh telemetry, and anything that cannot pass a guardrail is escalated rather than retried. The design ran unchanged on a local emulator and is defined entirely as code, so it can be reproduced and reviewed.

**RQ2: How does machine-learning anomaly detection compare with static thresholds?** The answer depends on the type of fault. For abrupt faults, such as a cooling unit failure or a rack hotspot, static limits were as fast as machine learning, because the fault crosses a limit immediately. For slowly developing faults, the LSTM autoencoder detected the problem three to ten times earlier, and it was the only detector that found pump degradation before it caused harm. Machine learning alone, however, missed stuck sensors and, in the case of the Isolation Forest, gross limit violations. The best results came from a hybrid that combines the model with the limits and a flatline rule; it detected all eight fault types without any automated false alarm and with about one notify-only alarm per day. On real pump data, the same detector matched the limits and the best published detectors in F1 score but raised more false alarms. The advantage of machine learning is therefore earlier detection of slow faults, not higher accuracy in general.

**RQ3: How much does automated remediation reduce the time to recover, and at what latency, scalability and cost?** In the simulated hall, automation reduced the median time to recover from 11–23 minutes to 1–13 minutes for the faults that could be handled without approval, and combining it with ML detection reduced it further to 0–2.3 minutes. The result held when the human response time was varied from 5 to 30 minutes, with message loss of up to 20 %, when a sensor in the same zone had already failed, and at milder and harsher fault severities, where the ranking of the modes never reversed. Automation could not shorten recovery from high-risk faults whose detection was already fast, because the approval of a person set the pace, and it could not resolve faults beyond the reach of its playbooks, such as a hotspot caused by a severe local loss of airflow. On the local stack, the cloud path added about 0.1 seconds, and in the live check on AWS the first command reached the gateway 2.4 seconds after the confirming sensor message, so the response time was set by the 30-second confirmation window of the detector, not by the cloud. With the usage measured on AWS, the cost model estimated about $0.25 per rack per month, or about $5 for a hall of 20 racks. On AWS, the pipeline served 2,000 gateways, about 400 data halls, with a 95th-percentile latency of at most 100 milliseconds, no lost messages and no throttling, using at most 43 of the 400 concurrent Lambda executions allowed.

## Comparison with previous research

The detection results agree with earlier findings on facility data. Huang et al. (2019) found that fixed thresholds miss level shifts and slow changes in data center infrastructure data, and the same weakness appeared here with pump wear and drifting sensors. Holly et al. (2022) found that autoencoders detect anomalies in cooling systems consistently but combined them with expert rules to locate faults, which corresponds to the hybrid detector and the rule-based diagnosis used in this thesis. The SKAB results agree with the conclusion of Schmidl et al. (2022) that simple methods are often competitive with deep learning: statistical limits reached almost the same F1 score as the LSTM autoencoder. The value of the learned model was not a higher score on a benchmark but earlier detection of the specific faults that matter for automation.

The remediation results extend self-healing research from software to physical equipment. Surveys of self-healing systems describe the MAPE-K loop and predefined recovery actions as the dominant pattern (Psaier & Dustdar 2011; Schneider et al. 2015), and the framework follows the same pattern. What differs at the facility layer is the cost of a wrong action: restarting a container is cheap, while moving the load of a UPS is not. The risk tiers, approval flow and rollback rules developed in this thesis are a practical application of the levels of automation proposed by Parasuraman et al. (2000), and the results confirm their trade-off: the approval step kept people in control of high-risk actions, and it was also the step that limited how fast those faults could be resolved. The recent review of AI-driven self-healing points to a validation gap for real deployments (AI-driven self-healing across the edge–cloud continuum 2026); this thesis addresses part of that gap with a quantitative, reproducible evaluation, although still in a simulated plant.

Compared with research on AI for facility operations, which optimises cooling during normal operation (Gao 2014; Zhan et al. 2025), the framework addresses a different problem: containing faults. The two are complementary. An optimising controller keeps the hall efficient when everything works, and a self-healing layer limits the damage when something breaks.

## Reliability and validity

The largest limitation of the work is that the plant was simulated. The faults had idealised shapes, each was injected into one target, and the simulator's parameters were plausible values rather than measurements from a real facility. The results therefore show what the framework does in a simulated hall, not what it would do in a specific real one. Several measures reduced this limitation. The model was checked for energy balance by a test, the detectors were validated on independent real data from a pump testbed, and E5 repeated every fault type at three severity levels without changing the ranking of the modes. The claims of the thesis have also been limited accordingly: the recovery times are relative comparisons between modes in the same simulated conditions, not predictions of recovery times in a real facility.

The internal validity of the comparisons was protected by the paired design, in which all modes faced exactly the same scenario, load pattern and operator response time, and by fixing the fault definitions, thresholds, playbooks, operator model and seeds before the final experiment batch. The batch was deterministic, and repeating it gave identical results, so the results are reproducible from the published code. The simulated operator is a source of bias. It responded without errors, fatigue or misdiagnosis and used the same playbooks as the automation, which favours the alert-only mode; the real advantage of automation may therefore be larger than measured. The sensitivity analysis showed that the conclusions did not depend on the assumed response time.

The detectors were trained and tested on data from the same simulator, which could overstate their accuracy. This risk was reduced by training on normal data only, using disjoint seeds for training, calibration and testing, and validating on SKAB. On SKAB the ML detectors raised more false alarms than the limits, which is reported openly; in the simulated hall the hybrid rules and the three-window confirmation kept automated false actions at zero, but a real facility may produce more varied normal behaviour than the simulator.

Two further limitations concern the cloud. The main experiments ran in an offline closed loop and on an emulator, so latency and cost on real AWS could only be confirmed by the final measurements. The live check on AWS confirmed the local results for a single hall: the pipeline behaved as on the emulator, the gateway-to-detector latency was below 0.1 seconds on average, and the first command reached the gateway 2.4 seconds after the confirming sensor message. ## Ethics and the use of artificial intelligence

The study used only simulated data and a public dataset and involved no human participants or personal data, so no research permits were needed. The SKAB dataset was used under its licence and cited. Security was considered part of research ethics: the framework uses per-device certificates, least-privilege roles and encryption, and the published repository contains no credentials or account identifiers. The thesis also has an ethical dimension in its subject. Automated control of critical infrastructure can cause harm if it is trusted beyond the evidence. For this reason the results are framed as results from a simulated hall, high-risk actions always keep a person in the loop, and the limitations above are stated explicitly.

Generative artificial intelligence was used during the work. Claude Code, an AI assistant developed by Anthropic, was used as a programming assistant for implementing and testing parts of the framework, and for drafting and editing text, including parts of this report (Anthropic 2026). The author defined the research problem, the design and the experiments, reviewed the generated code and text, and is responsible for all content. AI-generated code was accepted only when it passed the automated tests, and every number reported in this thesis was taken from the result files produced by the experiments, not from the assistant. The use of AI also had clear risks. The assistant occasionally proposed claims or references that could not be verified; these were removed, and references were checked against their original sources where these were accessible. A further risk is that the ease of generating text and code can reduce the author's own learning; this was addressed by working through the design and analysis decisions personally and by documenting them in the repository. The use of AI is reported in accordance with Savonia's reporting instructions.

## Applicability and significance

The results are relevant to data center operators, facility engineers and cloud engineers. For operators, the main message is that the largest gain comes from automating well-understood, reversible responses, such as starting a standby cooling unit or quarantining a faulty sensor, while high-risk actions benefit most from fast detection and a prepared plan that a person can approve quickly. The playbook pattern, the risk tiers and the guardrails can be adopted independently of the cloud platform, for example in an on-premises management system.

For cloud engineers, the framework is a reference architecture built only on actively supported AWS services, defined as code, and published with its tests and experiments. It shows how device shadows, Step Functions task tokens and DynamoDB conditional writes can be combined into a safe remediation workflow, and its estimated running cost is small compared with the value of the equipment it protects. The open-source simulator and experiment harness can also be reused to evaluate other detectors or playbooks under the same controlled conditions.

## Further development

Several directions would strengthen and extend the work. The most important is a pilot with real data. Connecting the detection layer to historical or live data from a real building management system, initially in an advisory mode that only proposes actions, would show how the detectors and diagnosis rules behave with real noise and real faults. Calibrating the simulator's parameters against such data would improve the external validity of the experiments.

On the technical side, an edge fallback, for example with AWS IoT Greengrass, would let critical playbooks run locally if the connection to the cloud is lost. The detection floor of 30 seconds could be lowered by sending messages more often for critical assets, at a known increase in cost. The diagnosis rules are transparent but hand-written; learning them from labelled incidents, or using a large language model to explain unexplained anomalies to the operator, are promising extensions, provided that the automated actions stay within the same guardrails. Finally, the approval flow could be integrated with an organisation's single sign-on and incident management tools.

## Thesis process and professional growth

The thesis was carried out as a series of steps, each ending with a working, tested result: the cloud foundation, the simulator, the telemetry pipeline, detection, remediation, guardrails, the experiments and the AWS validation. This incremental approach kept a working system available at every stage and made it possible to change the design when tests revealed problems. Examples include the hybrid detectors, which were introduced when the pure models missed stuck sensors, and the incident grouping, which was introduced when a single pump fault produced more than twenty separate alerts. Running the main experiments offline and on an emulator kept the cost of the work close to zero. In hindsight, the AWS measurements could have been scheduled earlier, so that their results would have been available while writing rather than at the end.

The work developed the author's competence in several areas at once. Designing the architecture required a practical understanding of cloud services, security and infrastructure as code. Building the simulator required learning the basics of data center cooling and power systems. The detection work involved training and evaluating machine-learning models and, equally importantly, understanding their limits. Designing the experiments and analysing them with non-parametric statistics was the most demanding part, and it changed the author's view of how evidence for a technical design should be produced: not by demonstrating that a system works once, but by comparing it fairly and repeatedly with the alternatives. These skills are directly applicable in cloud and infrastructure engineering work.
