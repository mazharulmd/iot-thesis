**Choice of topic**

The topic is self-initiated and has no commissioner yet. Potential commissioners are data center
operators, colocation providers or cloud consultancies that monitor facility equipment. The topic
comes from two observations. Industry outage surveys (Uptime Institute) show that power and
cooling failures are still the main cause of costly data center outages, yet most facilities
respond to them by hand: an alarm fires, an engineer investigates, then acts. At the same time,
AWS retired IoT Analytics, IoT Events and Lookout for Equipment in 2025, so there is no current
reference design for this kind of automation on AWS.

[One or two sentences on why the topic interests you and how it relates to your current or future work.]

The aim is to design, build and evaluate a serverless framework on AWS that detects faults in
data center cooling and power equipment from IoT telemetry and corrects them automatically and
safely: high-risk actions need human approval, and every action is verified and can be rolled back.

Research questions:
1. How can a serverless AWS architecture turn data center IoT telemetry into safe, automated remediation?
2. How does machine-learning anomaly detection compare with static thresholds in detection rate, time to detect and false alarms?
3. How much does automated remediation shorten recovery compared with alert-only manual response, and at what latency, scalability and cost?

**Thesis implementation**

The thesis is research-oriented development work following Design Science Research: the framework
is built as an artefact and evaluated in controlled experiments.

1. Literature review of data center monitoring, cloud IoT, anomaly detection and self-healing systems.
2. A simulated 20-rack data hall with cooling units, pumps, UPS and power distribution, which
   publishes IoT telemetry and lets faults be injected with known ground truth.
3. The cloud pipeline as infrastructure as code (AWS CDK): IoT Core, Lambda, DynamoDB,
   EventBridge, Step Functions, SNS and CloudWatch.
4. Anomaly detection: static thresholds compared with Isolation Forest and LSTM-autoencoder
   models, validated on the public SKAB pump dataset.
5. Remediation playbooks with safety guardrails: asset locks, risk tiers, human approval,
   verification, rollback, circuit breaker and rate limits.
6. Evaluation: fault-injection experiments comparing alert-only, threshold-based automation and
   ML-based automation across eight fault types and three severity levels, plus latency, load
   and cost measurements on real AWS.

The methods are quantitative: eight hypotheses with pass criteria are defined in advance, and
results are reported as medians with bootstrap confidence intervals and compared with
Mann–Whitney U tests. Research material is generated by the simulator, taken from the public SKAB
dataset and measured on AWS. No human participants or personal data are involved.

Preliminary schedule: Sep–Oct 2026 literature review, prototype and offline experiments; Nov 2026
severity sweep and validation on real AWS (latency, load test, cost); Dec 2026–Feb 2027 writing;
Mar 2027 final revisions, seminar and maturity test. Code, data and results are kept in a GitHub
repository.

**Relevance of thesis project**

The project builds practical skills that are in demand as data centers grow for AI workloads:
cloud architecture on AWS; IoT device connectivity (MQTT, X.509 certificates, device shadows);
infrastructure as code; serverless, event-driven system design; machine-learning anomaly
detection; safe automation of physical equipment; experimental design and statistical analysis;
cloud cost analysis; and technical writing. It also gives domain knowledge of data center cooling
and power operations.
